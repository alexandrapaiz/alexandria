## 0. The finding that reframes the directive

The directive asks why self-improving systems research arrives by accident.
The answer is that almost everything arrives by accident, and the topic is
not the variable.

| measure | value | query |
| --- | --- | --- |
| papers in the corpus | 10,026 | `select count(*) from papers` |
| ever judged by the triage model | 400 (4.0 percent) | `triage_log where method not like 'rule:backfill%'` |
| stamped `index` by `rule:backfill`, unread, unscored | 3,709 | `method like 'rule:backfill%'` |
| never triaged at all | 5,917 | `triage_queue` |
| arrival rate, last 7 days | 28 to 649 a day, mean about 336 | `papers group by fetched_at::date` |
| model judgment rate, last 10 days | 10 to 40 a day | `triage_log` same split |

Arrival exceeds judgment by roughly twenty times. The queue does not drain,
it grows by about 320 papers a day, and its oldest waiting paper is from
2026-08-01. `rule:backfill` has stamped 3,709 papers `index` with no model
reading them and no score, which is why the corpus looks triaged and is not.

**And triage is not the only stage with a backlog.** The same query run at each
stage gives the funnel, and the last number is the one that decides what the
product can say:

| stage | done | waiting | rate |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| ingest | 10,026 papers | - | about 336 a day |
| triage, by a model | 400 | 5,917 | 10 to 40 a day |
| distill | 846 claims | 33 papers | 10 to 43 claims a day |
| interpret | 306 claims | **540 claims (63.8 percent)** | 5 to 14 a day |

Interpretation is where this stops being an efficiency problem. A claim with no
edges is invisible to a digest that selects on graph evidence, so 540 claims
are in the corpus and cannot reach a reader. Section 5 has the case that shows
what that costs.

This has a consequence for the two deliverables the directive asks for, and
it is the reason this brief leads with it rather than with the census. A
rubric change reaches only the papers that get in front of the model, which
is 4 percent of them. A source addition adds to a queue that is already
twenty times oversubscribed, and a high-volume category would make the
backlog worse while burying the topic it was added to surface. The one
mechanism in the pipeline today that changes what a named topic gets read is
`PRIORITY_TERMS` in `pipeline/triage.py`, which drains matching titles first
inside every tier. It was built for the reasoning directive and it is the
right instrument for this one.

Its limits are also measured. The reasoning backlog was 147 untriaged papers
on 2026-09-26, a number `pipeline/triage.py` prints every run and records in
its own comment. Today it is 177. The priority reorders the queue; it does
not add capacity, so a priority can lose ground while working exactly as
designed. Naming self-improvement in `PRIORITY_TERMS` without lifting
throughput would put the two priorities in competition for the same 20-odd
slots a day.

### One paper, two rows, and a queue slot spent twice

Found while deduplicating the census, and it is why section 1 quotes 92
distinct papers against 110 rows. `papers` holds 5,189 arXiv rows over **4,944
distinct arXiv base ids, so 236 base ids are duplicated** (4.8 percent). The
dominant shape is the one `sources.yaml` says should not happen: 180 of the 236
are an `a`-tier row and a `b`-tier row for the same paper, and 25 more are
`a-low` with `b`. The file's own legend defines tier `b` as a strong prior that
"upgrades a paper already seen in tier a". It is not upgrading anything; it is
inserting a second row.

The cost is concrete and this week's digest shows it. Harness-Zero
(`2609.24974`) and RRSI (`2609.24972`) each sit in the corpus twice: once at
tier b, `deep_read`, distilled, carrying the claims the issue was built from,
and once at tier a, never triaged, still waiting in `triage_queue`. The
pipeline will keep the tier-a twin of every paper it has already read forever,
against a queue that section 0 shows is twenty times oversubscribed. The
remaining 31 duplicated ids are same-tier pairs.

**The mechanism, and it is a one-line shape.** Of the 236 duplicated base ids,
**200 are exactly one row carrying an arXiv version suffix and one without it**:
`arxiv:2609.26457v1` beside `arxiv:2609.26457`. The arXiv fetcher stores the id
as arXiv returns it, with the version, and the Hugging Face daily-papers fetcher
stores it bare, so an equality check between them never matches and the upgrade
the legend describes cannot fire. Normalizing the version suffix on insert is
the whole fix, and it makes the tier-`b` upgrade possible for the first time.

This is distinct from the `duplicate rate` metric in
`docs/product/graph-quality.md`, which samples claim text and passed its bound
tonight at 0 of 200. That metric asks whether two claims say the same thing.
This is the same paper stored twice, which no current metric counts.

## 6. Synthesis: where this is heading

Three weeks of this corpus point at one migration, and it is not the one the
headlines are having. The argument in September was about how much scaffolding
an agent needs. The argument that actually moved is about **where the
scaffolding lives**, and the field has stopped treating the harness as
furniture around a model and started treating it as a thing that is trained,
searched, or absorbed. That is a different claim from "agents need better
tooling," and it has a measured result behind it: Harness-Zero's distilled
model beats the harness it learned from, 44.3 against 41.7 (claim 668), which
says the scaffold's value can be moved into weights and the scaffold then
discarded. A year of agent engineering has assumed the opposite, that the
scaffold is where the leverage permanently sits.

What is compounding is the pairing rather than any single paper, and the three
results disagree in a way that is useful. RRSI evolves a harness and spends its
effort on not overfitting the benchmark it is scored against (claim 664).
Harness-Zero distils a harness into the model and drops it (claim 668).
Co-Evolving Harnesses finds that the obvious way to do that, imitation on
expert trajectories, makes the weaker model worse (claim 200), and that
rewriting only the failing turn restores it (claim 202). Read together they
describe one pipeline with three contested stages, which is the shape a claim
graph should be able to adjudicate and currently cannot: only 85 of 274 edges
join two different papers, so the corpus holds the disagreement without
recording it.

What is noise is the word. The census found 110 keyword matches that reduce to
92 papers, of which a substantial share are domain applications using a
refinement loop as a component, plus a steady weekly arrival of mathematical
analysis papers where `self-improving` is standard terminology for an estimate
that bootstraps its own regularity. Counting papers by the adjective overstates
the field by a wide margin; counting by "is there a closed loop with a measured
outcome" is what the rubric shipped in section 2 asks triage to do.

The connection to previous weeks is a narrowing, not a turn. The 2026-09-26
brief read reasoning training as the live front, and verifiable reward as its
engine. Harness evolution is the same engine pointed at a different object: RRSI
searches over scaffolds with a selector that rejects benchmark leakage, which is
reward-hacking defence applied to a harness instead of a policy. The reason this
matters for what gets built here is that the two priorities share machinery, so
a skill about one is partly a skill about the other, and the corpus's reasoning
claims and harness claims should be linked and are not.

**What a builder should do differently this week, on this evidence.** If you are
running harness evolution, put the selector before the search: RRSI reports its
gains alongside 30 percent fewer policy tokens than unregularized evolution
(claim 662), and its selector rejects candidates that leak benchmark content or
fail a noise-adjusted floor (claim 664). The paper reports the two together and
this brief does not claim the second caused the first. If you are considering distilling a
scaffold away, do not fine-tune on expert trajectories to get there; claim 200
says that regresses the weaker model by disrupting the model-harness fit, and
claim 202 says on-policy correction of the failing turn preserves it. And if you
are stacking post-training stages, ACLArena's numbers are the caution worth
carrying: single-hop search fell from 45.2 to 14.6 across stages and the final
stage recovered it only to 33.5 (claims 694 to 696). None of these is settled;
all three are single-team results, which is what the corpus can support this
week and the reason none of them is a skill yet.

## 7. What is rising, ranked by evidence

Ordered by traction and evidence, not by date, per the relevance law.

1. **An agent that rewrites its own code, measured over eight days.**
   `arxiv:2609.26457`, claims 702 to 705, distilled 2026-09-24 and
   uninterpreted. It leads on evidence rather than on novelty: seven discovered
   code upgrades, a discovered agent matching a human-engineered production
   research agent on four held-out benchmarks, and a reward-hacking rate falling
   from 55 to 32 percent inside the run. That last number is the one to carry,
   because it is the first result in this corpus where a self-improvement loop
   is shown getting *safer* as it improves rather than only better.
2. **Harness distillation into weights.** Compounding, and the week's
   strongest result: Harness-Zero's distilled model beats the harness it was
   distilled from, 44.3 against 41.7 (claim 668). Claims 667 to 671, five
   claims, `deep_read`, tier b.
3. **Regularized harness search.** RRSI, claims 662 to 666. The finding that
   matters for a builder is the selector that rejects benchmark leakage
   (claim 664), because it is the failure mode anyone running harness
   evolution hits first.
4. **Self-evolving skill libraries.** Not in the corpus at all, and the
   product's own territory. Four papers named in the reading queue by the
   skill seat: CoEvoSkills, SkillDAG, EvoSkill, Trace2Skill. Section 7.
5. **Co-evolution of harness and model.** Claims 199 to 203, and the most
   directly usable result of the four: imitation on expert trajectories
   regresses the weaker model (claim 200) while on-policy correction of only
   the failing turn restores it (claim 202).
6. **Interference across training stages.** ACLArena, claims 694 to 696.
   A caution for anyone stacking post-training stages, not a technique.

Rising authors and institutions: not reported this week. The
`discovery_report` signals for both read thin by their own documented caveat
until the corpus has 60 to 90 days of history, and `papers.institutions` is
populated only for distilled papers, which is 195 rows.

**What looked hot and is noise.** Volume on the self-improvement keywords is
inflated by two families that a reader of the count would mistake for
research: mathematical analysis, where self-improving estimates are standard
usage, and domain applications that use a self-refinement loop as a component,
of which the census found 13 in cs.CV alone. The census pattern is written to
be reproduced, so this is a caveat on the number, not a hidden correction.

## 8. Radar note for the writer and the skill seats

**For the skill seat, Tuesday.** The extraction target with the most support
is not the strongest single paper, it is the pairing that disagrees
productively:

- **Harness evolution, and what survives being distilled away.** Claims 662
  to 671 plus 199 to 203. Thirteen claims across three papers with a real
  tension in them: RRSI evolves and regularizes a harness, Harness-Zero
  distils one into weights and discards it, and Co-Evolving Harnesses finds
  that the naive way of doing that regresses the model. A skill that says when
  to evolve a scaffold, when to distil it, and which supervision preserves the
  model-harness fit has procedure, tradeoffs and a measured failure mode,
  which is the format's whole requirement.
- **Ask the engineer to interpret claims 702 to 705 before Tuesday.**
  `arxiv:2609.26457` is the single best extraction target in the corpus for this
  directive and it has no edges, so a cluster search will not surface it. It is
  four claims from one paper, so it is not a cluster on its own, but paired with
  the harness-evolution claims above it is the empirical anchor the skill would
  otherwise lack: the other five papers evolve or distil a scaffold and measure

## 14. The directive's premise, corrected

## 17. The finding that matters most, and it is not a paper

Following the MCP spec's SEP feed to its source: **SEP-2640, "Skills
Extension", status Final, created 2026-04-23.** It standardizes serving Agent
Skills over MCP — the `skill://` URI scheme, `skills/list` and `skills/get`,
extension identifier `io.modelcontextprotocol/skills` — and delegates the skill
format itself (directory shape, YAML frontmatter, progressive disclosure) to
the **Agent Skills specification** at `agentskills.io/specification`, requiring
clients to honour that spec's backward-compatibility mechanisms.

alexandria's terminal asset is a skills library. There has been a Final
standard for how skills are served, and a separate standard for how they are
formatted, for five months. Neither is in `sources.yaml`. The corpus holds zero
claims about either. The single feed we point at that repository reports
version dates.

Checked rather than assumed: `skills/*/SKILL.md` here already carries YAML
frontmatter with `name` and `description`, the Agent Skills specification's
stated minimum. **The library is shaped to be servable under SEP-2640 without a
format change.** What was missing is that nothing in this system knew the
standard existed.

**To the PM and the skill seat**, because it is not this seat's to act on: read
SEP-2640 and `agentskills.io/specification`, and decide whether conformance is
a launch item for 2026-10-13. A skills library that speaks the standard
interface is a different product from one that does not, and the standard is
Final, not draft.

**Signal read for these threads.** The world standardized the skills interface
and the agent-security frameworks in the last five months (SEP-2640 Final;
OWASP's 2026 Top 10 and its new Agent Control Standard). The evidence says our
security claims are good and unlabelled, and our protocols and containment
claims do not exist. The corpus is thin on protocols and empty on containment;
ingest there — cs.SE, the spec changelogs, and gVisor. The latest market brief
(`docs/market/briefs/2026-09-25.md`) names no steer on any of the three, and
