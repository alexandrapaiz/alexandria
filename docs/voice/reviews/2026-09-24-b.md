# Editorial review, 2026-09-24 (second run) — issue 2026-W39

Graded artifact: `site/content/issues/2026-W39.md`, the exact digests
record, published to the site this morning. Graded against the full canon
including law 13, which the owner ruled today and which this run promoted
into `docs/voice/canon.md`.

**The ruling being graded against is one day old.** Law 13 did not exist
when this issue was written, so the opening is not the generator
disobeying. It is the generator obeying `prompts/digest.md`, which told
it to do exactly this. That distinction is the finding, not an excuse,
and section 6 is about it.

## The single biggest weakness

The issue is well argued and badly served by its own machinery.

Its thesis is real, it is stated in the opening, and all four sections
carry it. That is the hardest thing on this list and W39 does it. What it
then does is break four rules that were live, on main, in the generator
that wrote it, roughly thirty times: ten em dashes, seven semicolon
joins, twelve non-ASCII characters, and nine papers discussed in prose
with no link to any of them.

So the weakness is not in the prose. It is that **nothing between the
rule and the reader is load-bearing.** Five editorial runs of prompt
fixes sit unmerged. The one mechanical gate that checks an issue before
it ships sits unmerged too, and when run against this issue it reports a
pass. An editorial system with three enforcement layers published an
issue that fails all three, and every layer was silent.

## 1. Pass one, the outsider read (law 12a)

Grades nothing. Read start to finish at reading speed, as a builder who
has read none of these papers and none of alexandria.

**Where I had to read twice.** The title. "Harness distillation without
the harness at runtime" is eight words and three of them are club words.
I did not know what a harness was, what distilling one meant, or what
runtime had to do with it, and the issue does not tell me for another
four paragraphs.

Then the wall in section two: "a 2B/4B/8B vision-language-action model
trained through multi-stage supervised fine-tuning: high-resolution
single-step grounding, then low-resolution multi-step trajectories across
all domains, then per-domain RL specialists, then rejection-sampling
distillation back into a unified model, finally joint asynchronous RL
with explicit cross-domain distribution balancing." One sentence, six
undefined terms, five stages. I did not read it twice. I skipped it, and
that is worse.

**Where following it needed something nobody handed over.** "Ablations
were brutal." "Macro-average." "Trajectories." "Zero-shot." "OOD gains."
"L1-style structural pruning." "Cosine schedule." "Off-policy drift."
"RL", which is never once expanded. "Model weights." "τ^τ-Bench", which
arrives as a Greek letter raised to a Greek letter. None of these gets a
plain-words clause anywhere in the issue.

**Would a person have finished it?** No. Five paragraphs run 143, 153,
167, 175 and 191 words with no break inside them. The density rule asks
for two or three sentences and then air. The longest paragraph here is
roughly nine sentences carrying four findings, eight numbers and a
caveat, and the reader has to unpack it rather than read it.

**What survived the read, and it is a lot.** The argument. "Three labs,
one bet: the future agent carries its training structure inside it, not
around it" is a real thesis, and I could still state it after the parts I
skipped. The evidence grades are the best thing on the page. The section
headings are written, not selected, and one of them is very good. An
outsider finishes this issue knowing less than they should and still
knowing the one thing that mattered.

## 5. The dispatch: law 13, and where the sentence came from

She struck one sentence: "You spent last week watching agents get faster
by doing less at test time." Her words: "dont assume readers read each
issue."

The generator asked for it. `prompts/digest.md`, in the opening spec:

> Some weeks the honest move is continuity: name what the last issue
> flagged as unresolved and say what changed, which orients and
> interprets in one move.

The rejected sentence is that instruction executed correctly. It names
what the last issue covered and says what changed, and it orients and
interprets in one move. The writer did what the file said.

That is ban list 44, added yesterday: a tell a model reaches for by habit
shows up in some issues, and a tell its instruction requires shows up in
all of them. Entry 44 was written from a second generator in someone
else's pull request. Twenty-four hours later the same defect is in the
file this seat owns, which is the one place entry 44 did not think to
look. Recorded as incident 31.

What went into the generator, six edits:

1. The continuity shape is gone, replaced with the running thread stated
   whole. Serves law 13.
2. A second invariant beside the context-first invariant, carrying her
   rejected specimen and the repaired opening. Serves law 13.
3. The greeting's "you" may say what the reader is building, never what
   they have read. Serves law 13, and this is where the sentence landed.
4. The daily's traction signal no longer says "believed on Monday", which
   dates the corpus by a publishing day. Serves law 13.
5. The older-claim thread is restated in full, never referenced. Serves
   law 13 and law 5 together, because the thread is the product and only
   the pointer was ever the problem.
6. A hard gate before output, naming four shapes, the fourth being the
   bare continuation that names no issue and still assumes one. Serves
   law 13.

The repaired opening, which is also the positive example now in the
canon:

> Agents have been getting cheaper by thinking less at the moment they
> answer. That trick has a ceiling. This week the field went after
> something harder. Every reliable agent is wrapped in scaffolding,
> meaning the checklists and retry rules and approval steps a team builds
> around a model to keep it on track. Three labs asked whether that
> structure could be taught to the model itself and then thrown away.

One line longer. The thread survives, stated rather than referenced. A
first-time reader loses nothing, and "at test time" is gone as a bonus.

## 6. Structure watch: three layers, all silent (charter step 4)

This is the part that should not be fixed with more prompt prose, and
the charter says to file it rather than patch it a third time.

**Layer one: the generator on main has not changed since 2026-09-19.**
`prompts/digest.md` on `origin/main` last moved at commit c3b4c49,
2026-09-19 19:39. Every writer run since is in an open pull request: #55,
#62, #67, #71, #74, #81. Nine editorial runs have patched a file the
press has never read. W39 was written by the 2026-09-19 generator, which
is why it fails rules this seat fixed days ago.

**Layer two: the rules that were live were broken anyway.** This is the
harder half, because it is not a merge problem. The 2026-09-19 generator
already says, at line 132, "Never use an em dash as a stylistic break",
and names the parenthetical pair explicitly, and W39 printed that exact
shape. It says at line 136 "Never join two sentences with a semicolon",
and W39 has seven. It says "Plain ASCII punctuation, always", and W39 has
twelve non-ASCII characters. It says at line 521 "Every item cites its
paper with a markdown link", and the three prose sections name at least
nine papers and contain no link at all. The reread gate at line 608
restates all three punctuation bans one final time before output.

Four rules, stated up to three times each, in the file that wrote the
issue, violated about thirty times. Restating them a fourth time is not a
fix, and I have not tried to. The one prompt change I made here is the
link rule, and only because its failure was a specific and correctable
misreading: the rule's emphasis sits on the reading list, and the model
satisfied the emphasised instance. It now counts items against links.

**Layer three: the gate reports a pass.** `tools/check_digest_quality.py`
exists, is tested, and has been open in engineer PR #60 since 2026-09-20.
I ran it against W39 read-only, out of the repo, and it returns:

```
2026-W39.md: 0 blocking, 4 warnings.
  [warn] empty-intensifier (2x, ban list 5)
  [warn] bare-number (2x, ban list 24)
```

Zero blocking findings on the issue the owner rejected. Three causes, all
of them in the tool rather than in the issue:

1. **It parses zero items.** `parse_items` returns an empty list for
   W39, because W39's sections one and three are flowing prose rather
   than bold-led items. Every per-item rule therefore ran over nothing
   and said nothing: `citation-per-item`, `ends-on-citation`,
   `uniform-rhythm`, `uniform-length`. The gate found no items and did
   not report that it had found no items. Silence was read as approval.
   A checker that cannot parse its input has to say so and fail, because
   a green light on an unread file is worse than no light.
2. **The em dash is not in its character list.** `TYPESETTER` holds four
   entries: the non-breaking hyphen, two spaces and the multiplication
   sign. The em dash, banned by law 1 and by the generator three times,
   is not among them, and neither is τ. This is ban list 36 exactly, the
   gate that enumerates instead of asking. The generator learned this
   lesson on 2026-09-21 and its ASCII rule now asks whether every
   character is ASCII rather than listing offenders. The tool still
   lists. The fix is one predicate.
3. **Both intensifier warnings are false.** `INTENSIFIERS` matches the
   substring `"very "`, so it fires on "e*very* screen" and "e*very*
   team". W39 contains no empty intensifier at either line. A gate that
   cries wolf on "every" is a gate whose warnings get skimmed.

Filed to the engineer in `docs/ideas.md`. Not patched here: `tools/` and
`pipeline/` are outside this seat's writable surface, and the point of
charter step 4 is that the third prompt edit is the wrong instrument.

## 7. What I would tell her in one line

The words are in better shape than the plumbing. This issue argues a case
and grades its evidence honestly, which are the two things hardest to
teach, and it shipped with thirty violations of four rules because
nothing between the generator and the reader was actually holding.
Merging the writer chain and PR #60 changes more about tomorrow's issue
than anything I could write into the prompt today.
