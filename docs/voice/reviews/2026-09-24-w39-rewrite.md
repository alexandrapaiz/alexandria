# Agents scored higher with the checklist trained in than bolted on, 44.3% to 41.7%

*The latest in AI research, read in full and distilled weekly: what's new,
what's gaining acceptance, and what newer evidence has overturned.*

Good morning. Anyone who has shipped an agent this year has ended up
writing the same unglamorous thing twice: the scaffolding.

Scaffolding is the code wrapped around the model to keep it on track. The
checklists. The retry rules. The step where something checks the output
before it counts. It works, and it is the reason a reliable agent costs
what it costs, because all of it runs again on every single request.

Three labs spent this week asking whether that structure could be taught
to the model and then deleted. A team from Peking University, Google and
HKUST says yes, and has the margin to show for it. A group at Google
Cloud and Stanford made the tuning of that structure stop behaving like a
lottery. Apple trained one model that drives a desktop, a phone and a
browser without a separate policy for each.

One belief also fell this week, and it was about ceilings.

The bet underneath all of it is worth stating plainly, because every
section below tests it. The structure that makes an agent reliable
belongs inside the model, not around it.

## The structure around the model is moving into it

Scaffolding is expensive at runtime, so the field has been circling one
question for months. Can a model absorb the habits its scaffolding
enforces, and then run without it?

Three independent papers built on that idea this week, all on the same
claim from Peking University, Google and HKUST. The claim itself is old
news by now. Evolving the scaffolding around a weaker model lifts
performance on real company work. What this week added is the part that
makes it permanent.

Their pipeline is called Harness-Zero, and it trains the scaffolding's
habits into the model itself.

It finishes **44.3% of tasks with the scaffolding gone**, up from 23.3%
for the untrained model.

The same work scored 41.7% with the scaffolding still bolted on. Teaching
the structure in beat leaving it attached, and that is the part nobody
expected.

What did the teaching is the more useful half. Not copying a stronger
model's transcripts, which is the standard move. A second agent watched
each step the learner proposed, checked it against a reference answer the
learner never saw, and swapped in the right step when the learner was
wrong. The corrected run became the lesson.

That one choice carries the whole result. Learning from corrected runs
scored 30%. Every alternative the team tried, including raw transcripts
from a stronger model and reviews that only looked at the final answer,
landed between 3% and 15%.

This is the authors' own work across three kinds of task, spreadsheets,
multi-app tool use and scientific reasoning, and nobody outside the group
has reproduced it. Nothing in it tests a scaffolding agent weaker than
the model it is teaching, which is the case most teams would actually be
in. If it holds, the expensive structure moves into training and your
runtime gets cheaper.

*Harness-Zero: Harness Distillation via Agent-as-Harness* - [full text](https://arxiv.org/html/2609.24974)

Nothing older moved enough this week to be worth your attention.

## Three teams tried to make the trick repeatable

None of the work below has been checked by anyone outside the group that
did it. It is a week old.

**The tuning stops being a lottery.** Teams who evolve their scaffolding
automatically know the failure: the thing drifts, learns the benchmark
instead of the job, and grows. Researchers at Google Cloud AI Research,
Stanford, and Washington University in St. Louis put three brakes on it.

- **A shrinking edit budget.** Early rounds explore widely, later rounds
  are allowed to change very little.
- **A selector that throws candidates out**, for leaking the benchmark,
  for winning by noise, or for costing more than the gain is worth.
- **A pruner that deletes any component** that has not paid for itself
  over a fixed window.

The result is **14.1 points on the benchmarks it evolved against, and 4.7
on five it had never seen**, with 30% fewer tokens spent on the policy.

The gap between those two numbers is the thing to read. A method that
gains three times as much on its own training ground as on fresh problems
may be tightening what it already had rather than learning to generalize.

*RRSI: Regularized Recursive Self-Improvement of Agent Harnesses* - [full text](https://arxiv.org/html/2609.24972)

**One Apple model drives every screen.** Most computer-use agents are
trained per surface: one for the phone, one for the desktop, one for the
browser. Apple trained a single model, MintAct, across all of them at
once, plus the grounding step of finding the right button on a screen.

It scores **48.9 on OSWorld-Verified**, the standard desktop-control test,
matching or beating specialists of the same size on their own ground.

The finding that should change a roadmap is not the score. It is that
training across all the surfaces together beat training a specialist for
each, which is the opposite of what the field assumed.

The cost is buried in the method. Apple ran this with more than 200
desktop and 100 mobile environments going at once. These are Apple's own
numbers on Apple's own infrastructure, and that infrastructure is the
part most teams cannot copy.

*MintAct: A Unified Visual Agent for Digital Environments* - [full text](https://arxiv.org/html/2609.22083)

**A benchmark that asks agents to rebuild what they see.** Alibaba built
250 tasks across Ubuntu, macOS, Windows, Android and the web, each one
the same instruction: here is a running application, make it again.

The strongest model tested, GPT-6 Astra, scores 58.1% overall and passes
every programmatic test on 2.8% of tasks.

Those two numbers are the whole story. Agents copy what a screen looks
like far better than what it does, and a partial score on this benchmark
means the interface was reproduced and the behaviour underneath it was
not.

The useful part is the training signal. Fine-tuning on these rebuild
attempts gained up to 17.9 points on five unrelated benchmarks, on
Alibaba's own runs.

*RecreationWorld: Scalable and Verifiable Environments for Hybrid Computer-Use Agents* - [full text](https://arxiv.org/html/2609.22000)

## A ceiling fell, and four unified designs got simpler

Start with the number that turned out to be wrong.

The field had accepted that a reference implementation written by human
experts sets the ceiling for agent-construction tasks, at 82.2% success.
A hierarchical reinforcement-learning method for cooperative air combat
now reports an 87% win rate in high-fidelity simulation.

The domains are different and the comparison is loose. What broke is the
belief that an expert-written baseline is a wall, and that belief was
doing real work in how teams set their targets.

*DRG-MAPPO: Hierarchical Dynamic Role-Graph Multi-Agent Reinforcement Learning for Cooperative Air Combat* - [full text](https://arxiv.org/html/2609.11155)

The rest is not wrong so much as replaced, and all four replacements go
the same way, from one big model that does everything to a smaller piece
doing less.

- **World models that generated the pictures too.** Replaced by
  SyncWorld, which calibrates on a single episode of an unseen
  environment and then simulates it cold.
  [full text](https://arxiv.org/html/2609.09155)
- **Robot skill frameworks with selection, execution and checking in one
  loop.** Replaced by Show-Harness, which hands an ordinary
  vision-language model a small vocabulary of discrete actions and gets
  fine-grained control with no extra model capacity.
  [full text](https://arxiv.org/html/2609.10522)
- **Self-evolving task graphs that repair a flawed expert plan.**
  Replaced by correction that rewrites only the turns that failed,
  leaving the weaker model's own way of planning intact.
  [full text](https://arxiv.org/html/2609.09134)
- **Monolithic agents that fused perception, interaction and control.**
  Replaced across the board by modular designs that give up the
  unification and lose nothing measurable for it.

Four for four in one week is a pattern worth holding onto. When a paper
offers you a single architecture that handles everything, the burden is
now on the unification to prove it was necessary.

## One paper worth your hour

*ACLArena: Agent Continue Learning in Multi-stage Post-training* - [full text](https://arxiv.org/html/2609.23989). Read it if you are stitching
supervised fine-tuning, reinforcement learning and distillation into one
pipeline and have to decide what each stage is allowed to overwrite. It
is the framework the three results above assume and none of them explain.

The other two papers worth an hour this week are the two already taken
apart above, so the summary is the recommendation.
