## 0. The biggest weakness, stated first

**The issue this seat has now graded nine times is not the issue a reader
gets. Two different texts share the name 2026-W37, and the archive serves
the one the press never wrote.**

`digests` holds one row. Week `2026-W37`, model `openai/gpt-oss-120b`,
prompt `83a0aa3be13c`, written 2026-09-14 at 15:00 UTC, 8,602 characters.
Its first line is:

> # Richer feedback boosts long-horizon agents [September 7-13, 2026]

`site/content/issues/2026-W37.md` is a different text. It was committed on
2026-09-18 in `ac9698f` and corrected on 2026-09-19 in `c30d4fa`. Its first
line is:

> # alexandria digest — 2026-W37

The two disagree on 170 lines. They have different titles, different
openings, different item bodies and different pipeline counts at the foot.
One says 3,558 papers were read and the other says 3,431.

The archive serves the file. `site/lib/content.js` reads
`site/content/issues/*.md` from disk, `site/app/library/[week]/page.jsx`
generates the page from it at build time, and no branch in this repository
has a database-backed path. The module's own comment says one exists in
production, and I checked every branch before believing it: the word Neon
appears in that file exactly once, inside that comment. What the deployed
build does I cannot see from a runner, and that is worth saying rather than
inferring. What the repository does is unambiguous.

Both texts fail the laws in the places that matter most, and they fail them
differently, so the grade below has to say which text it is grading. The
published one opens on the product's own name and the ISO week code, which
is law 4, ban list 22 and ban list 14 in a single line. Neither carries the
standing close, so law 10 fails twice over.

The consequence for this seat is larger than either text. Eight editorial
runs of findings, and every patch that came out of them, were produced by
reading a text that no subscriber can reach. The instrument has been
pointed at the wrong artifact since it was built.

## 2. The outsider read

Read at reading speed, start to finish, as a builder from another team.
This pass grades nothing, per canon law 12a. I read the published text,
because that is the one a stranger meets.

**Where I had to read twice.** The first sentence under the first heading:
"Feedback-Enriched Environments let agents learn guidance and explore
without test-time cues." Three terms arrive before any of them is placed.
Nothing on the page says what a test-time cue is, and the phrase "learn
guidance" does not survive being read carefully.

**Where following it needed something nobody handed over.** "The reward is
scaled on a fixed global scale shared across all tasks." I build software
and I do not know what a critic is, what PPO is, what an actor is, or why
one gets a learning rate thirty times the other's. All four are printed
bare. The issue never says that a reward is a number a training loop gets
for doing well, which is the sentence the whole section rests on.

**Would a person have finished it?** No. The traction section is ten
bullets of near-identical length, each ending in a parenthesis containing a
number and the word "supports", which is a count of edges in a database
nobody outside this repository has heard of. I stopped reading them at the
fourth and scrolled. The reading list then runs nine entries, eight of which
open with a verb of presentation. By that point the issue has stopped being
written to anyone.

This is the failure the owner named on 2026-09-19 in her own words, "i feel
like an outsider to something privy", and it is still exactly right about
the text the site serves today.

## 4. The ten laws

One verdict each, on the published text, each carrying a quoted line.

**Law 1, plain sentences and no stylistic em dashes. FAIL.** "a set of
training tricks—dense verification rewards, token-stream alignment, and a
focused reinforcement-learning loop—lift a 122B Mixture-of-Experts terminal
agent". A parenthetical em dash pair around a definition, holding a
three-item list, inside a sentence that had already finished its work. The
database row carries five more em dashes in its source lines. Both texts
also fail the plain-ASCII half of the law, 152 non-ASCII characters in the
published file and 134 in the row.

**Law 2, no buzzwords, every line targeted. FAIL.** "Both currents promise
more reliable, self-sufficient agents that need less hand-crafted inference
scaffolding." Nothing in that sentence can be acted on or checked, and it is
the last thing the opening says.

**Law 3, sell the product and never the recipe. PASS on the published text.**
The row fails it in its tagline. The file carries no method narration.

**Law 4, the headline is a finding. FAIL, and this is the worst line in the
issue.** "alexandria digest — 2026-W37" names the sender and the week. It is
the email subject, the archive listing and the browser tab, and it contains
no finding, no number and no reason to open anything. The database row's
title is a real finding with a date bolted to it, so the better of the two
titles is the one no reader sees.

**Law 5, relevance is impact and the ranking says what earned each slot.
FAIL.** The new and unproven work is printed first, under "Trailblazing",
and traction comes second. That is her order reversed. Inside the traction
section the ordering key is "(3 supports)" and then "(2 supports)", which is
the raw count rather than what the evidence should change.

**Law 6, evidence grades in-line. FAIL.** "resolves 64% of tasks on the
standard benchmark" arrives with three PPO epochs, a 15k example dataset and
a baseline, and with no statement anywhere of how much weight the number
carries. One paper, one benchmark, the authors' own experiments, and the
issue never says so.

**Law 7, honesty serves items and never the method. PASS.** The corrections
note at the foot is dated, specific and about content rather than process,
and it names the three errors plainly. It is the best writing in the file.

**Law 8, links reach the full text. FAIL.** Every link in both texts is an
`arxiv.org/abs/` landing page. Twenty of them. The database row is worse
than the file, because its source lines read "[link]", so the last thing a
reader sees on an item is a word that names nothing.

**Law 9, the fine-tuned instruction is the foundation. FAIL.** Both texts
print all four of her internal slot names as headings, and the published one
adds "*Procedure*" and "*paper*" as italic labels inside items.

**Law 10, the dual-audience close. FAIL, both texts.** Neither ends on "You
read to decide. Your agents load to act." The published file ends on a
corrections note and the row ends on a line of pipeline counts.

**Law 11, length follows the news. FAIL.** Ten traction bullets, nine
reading-list entries, four sections, and every item in the first section
carrying a five-step numbered list whether the finding needed steps or not.

**Law 12a, the outsider test and density. FAIL.** Section 2 above is the
evidence.

**Law 12, framework names never print. FAIL.** "Trailblazing", "Gaining
traction", "Left behind", "Read these yourself", all four, in both texts.

Thirteen verdicts, two passes. The generator has since been patched against
every one of the eleven failures, which is the whole reason this grade is
worth less than it looks and the reason section 1 is in this review. A
failing grade on a text written by a prompt that no longer exists tells the
owner nothing about what her press would print tomorrow.

## 5. The ban list, and the sweep incident 26 briefed

Incident 26 closed yesterday with a brief for this run. Two gates in
`prompts/digest.md` had been rewritten to test a class instead of a list,
one per run, and the entry's own reading was that one-at-a-time was the
failure. This run swept the rest of the file.

The distinction that made the sweep tractable, and it is worth stating
because it is not obvious: the defect is a gate that enumerates **the
failures already seen**, never a gate that enumerates **the artifact in
front of it**. The first-use pass walks the issue's own terms and is sound.
The reread walked a memory of thirteen past tells and was not.

Six rules were enumerating. Each one is now a question, with its examples
kept underneath as evidence.

| Rule | The question it now asks | What the list was missing |
|---|---|---|
| Hedging and hype | Does this word add information or tell the reader how to feel about it? | The two were separate bans on one defect |
| Punctuation | Is this mark doing the job of a full stop? | The colon join, banned nowhere |
| Internal vocabulary | Could a subscriber who has never seen the codebase decode this word? | "ingested", printed in both texts |
| Verb of presentation | Does this verb belong to the paper or to the reader? | "provides", which shipped |
| The pre-output reread | Did a shape repeat where the material did not? Is anything asserted without the thing that makes it checkable? | Thirteen tells, no class |
| The heading gate's roll of thirteen labels | Already a question since run 7 | Trimmed, because the lesson now sits once at the top |

The lesson itself is now one paragraph at the head of the Rules section, and
it paid for itself: the roll of thirteen labels in the heading gate and the
second copy of the internal-vocabulary list in the sign-off both came out.
Net cost 850 bytes and 182 tokens.

**Ban list changes in this pull request.** Two new entries and two
amendments, all from evidence in the two texts.

- **39, the verb of presentation.** Eight of nine reading-list entries open
  with one. "Provides" is among them and is on nobody's list.
- **40, the mark doing a full stop's job.** The class that entries 37, the
  em dash rule and the semicolon rule are three instances of. It names the
  colon join, which is the instance currently banned nowhere.
- **14 amended.** The class stated as a question, with "ingested" named as
  the internal word that walked past a list holding supports, tiers, scores,

- **33 amended.** "*paper*" added as the fourth italic label found, because
  the entry names three and the issue prints a fourth.
