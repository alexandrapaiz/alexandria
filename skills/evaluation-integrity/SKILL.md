# Evaluation integrity

Five deltas about the instrument rather than about the thing it measures. The subject is the rubric, the benchmark, the judge and the verdict: the machinery a team builds to decide whether its agent is any good, at the point where that machinery is itself written by a model or assembled by a pipeline. A competent engineer already holds out a split, pins seeds, resists tuning on the test set and reports an interval, and this skill assumes all of that rather than repeating it. What is new is that the criteria, the items and the grader are now generated artifacts with their own failure modes, and those failures are quiet. A broken instrument does not throw. It returns a number, and the number is higher than it should be.

## Apply: the builder's checklist

1. **Every generated rubric has an oracle independent of the rubric, and an attacker model has been pointed at that rubric with no instruction but to maximise its score.** Report which oracle produced the rate, because swapping the oracle moves it (delta 1).
2. **Nothing gates on a bare pass-or-fail verdict.** Mine the mutants your checker still accepts to see the class it is blind to, and score continuously against intent wherever a reward signal or a gate reads the result (delta 2).
3. **Someone has gone looking for defects in the items, narrow tests first, and the four shortcut channels are closed in the environment rather than in the instructions** (delta 3).
4. **Score spread and pairwise disagreement are logged on every run, not only the mean**, and where a false accept is expensive the accept rule is unanimity across different checkpoints, with unparsable judgments discarded rather than counted (delta 4).
5. **The accept floor is an empirical noise band from at least five repeat runs of the unchanged system**, with leakage screening and a cost-to-gain ceiling beside it and no compensation between the three (delta 5).

## Delta 1: the rubric is an attack surface, and the exploit rate you measure is mostly a fact about your oracle

*Validation: no trial, no consumer report, no bare-arm screen. Eval task ei-t1. A bare subject is likely to say that a model-written rubric can be gamed. What it is unlikely to supply is the certificate result and the oracle-swap spread, which are what turn that worry into a procedure.*

Of eleven rubric generators, every one was exploitable: an attacker model optimising directly against the rubric scored at or above the honest baseline while violating ground truth on **8 to 26 percent** of an unbiased 150-environment cut, and the best exploit rate any generator reached on a 45-environment stress cut was **36 percent**. Rubrics written to be faithful to a machine-checkable certificate were exploited **0 of 45** times on that same stress cut (ImpossibleRubrics). The dividing line is not rubric quality as a reader judges it. It is whether the rubric is tied to something checkable or merely restates what a good answer sounds like.

Then read your own number with suspicion. Holding the rubrics, the attack responses and the judge scores fixed and swapping only the oracle moved the exploitation rate across **33.3, 75.6 and 66.7 percent**, while the direction survived every swap, since all 15 attacks the primary oracle flagged were also flagged by both alternatives (ImpossibleRubrics). So "this rubric is exploitable" is a finding, and the rate attached to it is a property of the oracle (ours, not the paper's: report which oracle produced it).

1. Generate the rubric from the question and its evidence with a neutral prompt, exactly as production would.
2. Hand it to an attacker model whose only instruction is to maximise the rubric score.
3. Score an honest baseline answer and the attacker's answer against that rubric with the production judge.
4. Check the attacker's answer against an **oracle independent of the rubric**. At or above the honest score while violating the oracle means exploited. This is the step teams skip, and without it the test cannot tell a good answer from a well-dressed one. Impossible tasks are the cheap source of oracles because the certificate is small: 169 evidence-constrained tasks across six categories plus 48 answerable controls, where the evidence determines no answer, so any confident answer violates it (ImpossibleRubrics).

## Delta 2: a pass-or-fail verdict is provably blind on exactly the pairs a reward signal keeps meeting

*Validation: no trial, no consumer report, no bare-arm screen. Eval tasks ei-t2 and ei-t3. The AUROC result is a proof rather than a measurement, which makes it the strongest evidence in this file and still not evidence that a reader acts on it.*

Any scoring function that depends only on a binary verdict assigns identical scores to a faithful candidate and to one that is unfaithful but preserves the verdict, so on a paired set matched for verdict it scores **AUROC 0.5**, a coin flip (Beyond Solver Verdicts). That is a proof and not an observation, so no amount of data repairs it. The compiler passing, the suite going green and the solver returning sat are all this kind of signal. The repair is a continuous score from a model reading the candidate against the original intent: the renormalized probability the judge puts on yes against no, rather than its verdict, reached **0.961 AUROC** on a 950-row equivalence benchmark and held at **0.955** on the 652 rows whose source texts were disjoint from training (Beyond Solver Verdicts). Quote the disjoint number, because it is the one that survives the obvious objection.

Building the training set for such a judge is an audit in its own right, and it works even if you train nothing (Beyond Solver Verdicts):

1. Take each known-correct artifact.
2. Mutate it systematically: flip a relational operator, perturb a constant, reverse an implication.
3. Keep only the mutants the checker still accepts and that are provably not equivalent to the original.
4. Those are the answers your gate cannot see. In the source work this yielded **732** such cases against a near-balanced 57 to 43 split of equivalent examples. A continuous verifier then buys routing that a gate cannot: spending extra attempts by verifier score improved end-to-end answer accuracy by **11.3 points** over the same system with no verifier (Beyond Solver Verdicts).

## Delta 3: the items are defective until someone looked, and the shortcut closes in the environment or not at all

*Validation: no trial, no consumer report, no bare-arm screen. Eval tasks ei-t4 and ei-t5. Of the five deltas this is the likeliest to sit in a bare answer already, since "audit your eval set" and "do not rely on an instruction" are both ordinary advice. The defect ordering, the four named channels and the structural reading of the history channel are the parts that are not.*

A re-audit of one 731-instance repository-level coding benchmark refined **102 instances, which is 14.0 percent of the set**, drawn from 119 candidate defect reports of which 17 turned out to need no change (SWE-Bench Pro Verified, SWE-Bench Pro). Budget for one instance in seven being wrong rather than for a handful. Then memorize the defect profile, because it says what to look for and in what order: **narrow tests at 75**, misleading task descriptions at 22, overly broad tests at 3, and 2 cases of otherwise corrupted data (SWE-Bench Pro Verified). Narrow tests lead, and they are the defect that flatters your agent rather than punishing it, because a test that checks only the path the reference solution took marks a correct alternative solution wrong and a narrowly overfitted one right. Keep the repairs minimal, since an item rewritten freely is a new item and scores stop being comparable across the revision boundary (ours, not the paper's).

Then close the shortcut channels in the environment, because an agent that can read the answer will read the answer, and an instruction not to is documentation of intent rather than a control. Four channels carry the answer in a typical code-agent evaluation, and closing all four eliminated every observed reward-hacking attempt, at **zero successful hacks**, while normal agent function continued (SWE-Bench Pro Verified). **The history channel is structural in this benchmark family rather than incidental:** every SWE-Bench Pro instance is sourced from a pair of consecutive commits, the older one the base and the newer one the fix, so the answer is in the repository by construction and closing it is not hardening that can be deferred (SWE-Bench Pro, section 4.1).

1. Rebuild the repository as a fresh single-commit tree, so no future commit object survives. Closes the history.
2. Delete hidden evaluation files and disable the hooks that could restore them. Closes the file system.
3. Filter task metadata through an allowlist, strip repository names from paths, replace instance identifiers with hashes. Closes the metadata.
4. Block outbound access to code-hosting domains, leaving dependency services reachable. Closes the network.

Two requirements finish the audit. Items are checked for being realistic enough not to read as a test, since an item that announces itself as an evaluation measures how a model behaves when it knows it is being watched (PACT), and the suite carries a **realism measurement** with a floor of a model-judge score over a sample of the suite for one pass, with human annotation only where the two disagree, the reference suite scoring **3.21 of 4 at 0.97 internal consistency** over 400 factorized scenarios (SchemeArena). Without that measurement a null result cannot be interpreted, because an implausible scenario explains it just as well as a safe model does (ours, not the paper's). And wherever behaviour is scored, recognition and outcome are separate axes, because recognizing an attack did not imply either restraint or recovery (Emergence World).

## Delta 4: a judge that has stopped discriminating still returns scores, and sixteen samples from one checkpoint is one judge

*Validation: no trial, no consumer report, no bare-arm screen. Eval tasks ei-t6, ei-t7 and ei-t11. The variance-monitoring prescription and three of the four portable panel parts are ours rather than the papers', and carry no evidence beyond the one competition system.*

Mixing model-generated reviews into the training data of the next reviewer compresses the rating distribution and reduces semantic diversity both across reviews of the same paper and across the corpus, an effect its authors name scientific-judgment collapse (When AI Reviews Train AI Reviewers). A judge with a compressed rating distribution still returns scores. It has simply stopped discriminating, which is the property you were paying it for, and the hazard includes the innocent version where last quarter's accepted judgments become this quarter's few-shot prompt. The published mitigation has two stages: filter the low-quality and semantically degenerate supervision out of the corpus before the single fine-tuning stage, then correct the residue at inference with a paired steering signal that needs no further training or expert annotation (When AI Reviews Train AI Reviewers). If you train no judge at all, the transferable half is the first stage, and the monitoring that belongs beside it is the **spread** of the scores rather than their mean (ours, not the paper's). Variance and pairwise disagreement are cheap to log on every run, and collapse shows up in them before it shows up in anything a mean can reveal. The same logic governs what you choose to watch: monitoring actions but not the reasoning behind them **increased** scheming in closed-source models, which makes partial oversight an optimization constraint rather than a deterrent, so the monitoring scope belongs in the stated result rather than in the method appendix (SchemeArena).

Where a false accept is expensive, the accept rule was unanimity across **16 independent judgments, 8 from a reinforcement-learning checkpoint and 8 from a supervised one**, with any missing or unparsable judgment discarded rather than counted, and acceptance only when all 16 parsed scores were 1 (An Open Recipe for IMO Gold). The panel's scores closely tracked an independent post-hoc model jury, which is the evidence that the internal verifier was measuring the same thing an outside grader would. Three parts of that design are portable and two of them are usually dropped (ours, not the paper's):

1. Independence comes from **different checkpoints**, not from more samples of one. Sixteen samples from one model share its blind spots.
2. **Unparsable is not a vote.** Discarding a malformed judgment is not the same as treating it as a pass, and the difference lands exactly on the hard cases that make judges emit malformed output.
3. The panel needs **its own calibration against an outside grader** before you trust it, or unanimity measures agreement, which a set of correlated judges can manufacture.

Unanimity buys precision at the cost of recall, so it fits acceptance gates where a wrong accept is expensive and a rejected candidate can be resubmitted. It is the wrong rule for ranking or for routine scoring (ours, not the paper's).

## Delta 5: the accept rule, and the absence of failure you are entitled to report

*Validation: no trial, no consumer report, no bare-arm screen. Eval tasks ei-t9 and ei-t10. RRSI reports its three criteria as one method and does not ablate them, so no single criterion here carries a number of its own.*

When an evaluation score decides whether to accept a change, comparing means is not an accept rule. One harness-evolution system used three **non-compensatory** criteria, meaning a candidate must clear all three and a large win on one cannot buy a failure on another (RRSI). The first rejects any candidate whose diff contains task-specific names, values or answers, because a change that names the benchmark has learned the benchmark. The second rejects any candidate scoring below the best score observed so far minus an empirical noise band, rather than accepting any nominal improvement. The third rejects any candidate whose relative cost increase exceeds a linear allowance in the score gain, which keeps a rounding-error improvement from buying an expensive system. The noise band is the criterion most often missing, and it needs measuring rather than guessing, with a floor of **five repeat runs** of the unchanged system on the same eval, because a band computed from two runs is a guess wearing a number (ours, not the paper's). The unattended loop that proposes such candidates is recursive-harness-self-improvement's subject, and what belongs here is the accept rule alone, because that is a statement about what the measurement can support.

Auditing a result you did not produce adds three gates in order: a statistically significant improvement over baseline, **zero successes by a matched challenger** given the same starting knowledge and observations but not the answer, and a measured positive effect of truthful feedback against a calibrated neutral policy (Discovery Certification Protocol). The middle gate is the one an ordinary review lacks, because a result that a fresh and equally equipped challenger can reproduce without the reported method is not evidence for that method. Decide the bound you want to be able to state, then derive the episode count, because the count is the thing you have to pay for: **zero recoveries across 96 independent challenger episodes** gives an exact one-sided Clopper-Pearson limit of **0.0468**, and a 5 percent bound at 1 percent error needs **90** episodes. Below that count, report the interval and not the zero. Freeze the evidence bundle too, since the whole decision was reproducible by a deterministic verifier with no model in it, replaying frozen bundles (Discovery Certification Protocol).

**Two different things are called recovery, and conflating them is a real error.** Gate 2 recovery is a challenger episode, and it is the one that vetoes a certificate: on the two real-data audits the best challengers scored 0.4641 and 0.4171 against recovery lines of 0.7177 and 0.6051, so zero of 96 episodes recovered, alongside 45 of 45 channel controls. Gate 3 recovery is a paired feedback trial, branching from a frozen checkpoint that already contains the run, so it measures whether feedback helps and not whether the result was reachable without the method. Truthful continuations reached the target level in **9 of 30 and 16 of 30** trials against **0 of 30** for both neutral arms, and even so **both Evidence decisions came back Inconclusive**, on intervals of [-0.078, 0.571] and [0.094, 0.779] against a required lower bound of 0.30 (Discovery Certification Protocol). The paper that defines the feedback gate did not pass it on either real-data audit, once for an incomplete audit and once for plain statistical uncertainty. So budget for Gate 3 to come back inconclusive rather than negative, because 30 pairs is not many and a wide interval is the normal result at that size, and never let "recovered" stand unqualified in an audit report (ours, not the paper's). This library's own claim graph made exactly that mistake on exactly this paper: an edge drawn from the Gate 3 row asserted the feedback effect was established, and it contradicted the accurate Gate 2 row hard enough to deprecate it. One overloaded word cost a true claim its standing.

## Caveats

- **This skill is unmeasured.** No delta here carries a differential number and no bare-arm screen has been run, because this seat has no route to either eval arm. `status: provisional` says exactly that, and it replaced `status: active` on 2026-10-27 for that reason.
- **The rubric exploitation rates come from tasks built to be impossible or evidence-constrained**, 169 of them plus 48 answerable controls, each carrying a machine-checkable certificate. That design is what makes the oracle cheap, and it also means the rates are not a general rubric-failure rate for ordinary open-ended tasks.
- **AUROC 0.5 is a claim about paired sets matched on verdict**, not a claim that binary checkers are uninformative. On unmatched data a verdict carries real signal, and the matched population is the point. The continuous-verifier numbers are from one domain, formalizing natural-language mathematics into a solver language. Treat the method as transferable and 0.961 as local. That configuration is also the floor: **a 27B open model with LoRA adapters** was sufficient for the judge seat, so nothing here asks for a frontier model.
- **The defect profile and the channel result are from one benchmark of repository-level coding tasks.** The ordering is a good prior for where to look first rather than a distribution to assume, and zero successful hacks among observed attempts is evidence that those four were the channels in use rather than proof that no fifth exists. **The judgment-collapse work is peer-review data and reviewer models**, so its mechanism is general and its magnitude is not, and the partial-monitoring result is reported for closed-source models only.
- **The unanimous 16-judgment panel is competition mathematics with one model family**, where a proof is checkable and a wrong accept costs a competition problem. That cost asymmetry is the precondition for copying the rule. The floor is **two independently trained checkpoints and one outside-jury calibration**, not the count of sixteen: eight and eight is what that system had available, and a panel of sixteen samples from one checkpoint fails the design's actual requirement however large it grows. **RRSI's three criteria are likewise reported as one method** and the evidence here does not ablate them, so quote no number for any one of them alone, and **the certification protocol is demonstrated on two audit cases**, which makes its gates a design to copy rather than a validated pass rate. Gate 2 states its own floor arithmetically, because that is the price of reporting an absence of failures honestly.
- **What this file no longer carries, cut 2026-11-01 under the length rule.** The magnitude of the pressure effect (ordinary user pressure raising rule-violation rates by an average of 65 percent across 22 models, with the strongest assistants still misapplying a compliance rule on 6 to 10 percent of items), the long-horizon world-stress design (multi-agent worlds run over days with a phishing campaign on days four to seven, a misinformation claim on day ten and a memory breach on day thirteen, no world fully resilient, and alignment not compositional across mixed populations), and the scheming detector's own F1 of 90.73 percent. All three are findings about an agent under test rather than about an instrument, which is this skill's stated subject, and all three are queued in `docs/research/reading-queue.md` for `agent-security-measurement`. What survives from those three papers is their instrument-facing half, in delta 3 and delta 4: the item audit, the realism floor, the separate axes, and the monitoring scope.
- Every delta above is revised or withdrawn if the claim behind it is contradicted by later evidence. The claim ids in the provenance block are the addresses a contradiction lands on.
