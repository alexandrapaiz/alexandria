# Agent approval paths

Three findings about the human confirmation step in an agent's action loop, each one a place the measured evidence points away from the design a competent engineer reaches for first. The common root: an approval gate is a channel, and its security is set by what the human is shown and what authority survives the click, not by the fact that a human was asked. Asking is the cheap half and it is the half that gets built.

This adds to standard engineering practice rather than replacing it. Putting a human in front of a consequential action is already correct, and nothing below argues for removing the gate. All three findings are about the design of that step, and all three have the same shape: the obvious version of the control is measurable and measures far worse than it reads.

## Apply: the builder's checklist

1. **The confirmation text is generated from the complete descriptor of the action that will execute**, at the enforcement point, never from the agent's own summary and never from a label a fetched page controls. **Decoded-from is not the same requirement as complete, and only the first is what an engineer builds**: a display derived faithfully from the real action can still be a lossy projection of it, which is measured at 3 of 3 on a released gateway shell path that rendered only the inline payload of an argument vector already complete before approval. So enumerate the material fields of the surface and either show them or refuse to authorize: on a shell surface the measured list is inline payloads, positional arguments, environment changes, working directory, interpreter flags and redirections (delta 1).
2. **The approval binds to the complete resolved operation, not to the view, and the enforcement point reconstructs that operation from scratch at dispatch and compares** rather than re-checking the fields it stored, so neither changed state nor a field the record never held can substitute a different action after the click, and an action *class* is never what gets approved. **Binding a call identifier, or a run-level approved flag, or checking only at presentation time are each measured insufficient**, one per studied product. **Then validate, at approval time, what the environment already in place would make that exact action produce**, because a field-only binding is measured at no significant reduction on the two classes where the dispatched field equals the granted one: 1.000 to 1.000 on scope at 0 discordant runs of 20, and 0.450 to 0.400 on arguments at 1 of 20, both p of 1. Comparing an environment snapshot at dispatch is not the substitute and catches none of the measured cases (delta 1).
3. **Both burden numbers are measured before shipping**: confirmations per legitimate task and false-block rate, against a demonstrated floor of 0.94 and zero (delta 1).
4. **The uninspectable rate is the fatigue number, not the prompt rate**, measured on a normal-usage corpus you did not author, and anything the mediator cannot decode surfaces as uninspectable rather than being silently approved (delta 2).
5. **No persisted allow-rule is ever created from a scope proposed during a task**, and a mid-task request to persist one is handled as an alert, because benign runs proposed one in 0 of 100 pooled, bounding the organic rate at 3.0 percent pooled and 11.3 percent per model (delta 3).
6. **A persisted rule binds to the action and its resource, never to a command prefix**, and expires at the task boundary rather than being inherited across it (delta 3).
7. **Build the approval tier before the object binding**, because the tier is worth 18 of 74 attacks and the binding 2, and audit the resolver against the full alias list, where case matching and a race-free open are separate requirements rather than resolver properties (delta 1).
8. **If the gate consults effects, they are observed effects from a rolled-back pre-execution, judged outside the agent loop before the commit.** The safe twins a text-based gate spends are the price of deciding from text at all and not the price of prediction: predicting the effects costs **nothing** against the dialogue approver it replaces, and only observation buys the 60.8 points back. **Price the extra prompts the effect-bound record asks for before shipping it**, because on the one frozen holdout that measured them **9 of 17 reached cases asked for an approval that disclosed no effect the command text had not already disclosed** (delta 2).

## Delta 1: a confirmation step is not the control, and what the human is shown is

*Validation: none, and that is unchanged by this revision. No trial, no consumer report, no bare-arm screen (see `provenance.differential_screen`). Eval tasks ap-t1, ap-t4. What changed on 2026-11-09 is the evidence class, not the validation: three independent lines now reach this construction, which is the charter's bar for a pattern. Three sources agreeing is not a measured delta and this section still has none. **v6 adds two more lines that do not agree, and that is the stronger evidence:** they measure the ceiling of the half of the construction this section tells a builder to ship, so the section now carries a limit rather than only a recommendation. The ceiling sentences rest on `2609.38983` alone, which is a single-author working draft on one harness that disqualifies its own point estimates as harness-intrinsic, so take the direction and the two zeros rather than the rates. **v7 adds the precondition from `2609.21081`, which is a change of decision rule rather than a caveat**, and it rests on that paper alone: four purposive product paths, deterministic trial counts and not rates, one researcher, no independent reproduction. Its one strength the other two lack is that the repair is a released vendor fix measured against the affected release of the same product, which is more than either ceiling sentence has behind it. Eval task ap-t9 is written against it.*

Four arms differing only in the confirmation step, run across five open-weight models plus a separate GPT-4.1 run, 293 clean trials in the sweep plus 40 in that run (The Verifiable Action Card). With no gate, attack success is **78 percent** aggregate, 68 to 100 by model, at 80 percent task completion. With a naive confirmation, whose summary derives from a label the page controls, attack success is **72 percent** at one confirmation per task and a zero false-block rate. With approval rendered from the observed ground-truth action and re-verified at dispatch, attack success is **0 percent on all five models**, task completion is 78 percent, the false-block rate is **0 percent**, and the burden is **0.94 confirmations per legitimate task**.

So adding a human bought six points and changing what the human was shown bought the other seventy-two. The paper's provenance-stripped arm also scores 0 percent at 0.95 confirmations, which locates the effect in the rendering and the dispatch-time re-verification rather than in the provenance component, and the paper's own reading is that the protection is architectural rather than model-specific, since the check inspects the ground-truth action after the model has already decided.

**This is now a three-source pattern rather than a single-paper delta.** A third line reaches the same construction independently and cites neither of the other two: ZTAC resolves every requested name to the kernel object before it classifies, renders the approval from the resolved operation rather than the requested one (showing `read .env` for a request that named `config.txt`), rebinds at consumption, expires approvals in ten minutes and consumes them on first use. It is implemented, 3,164 lines of trusted Rust, and measured on files, programs, git state and endpoints rather than on a DOM: **2 of 74 attacks succeed, against 66 of 74 for a tool allowlist and 56 of 74 for an allowlist plus the command sandbox deployed agents ship**, all 10 replayed disclosed incidents contained, at **43 of 46 benign operations with 8 prompts** and a 4.0 ms median brokered request.

**Its ablation table reorders which half of the construction pays, and the order is not the one the construction's name suggests.** Removing the approval tier admits **18** of the 74, the largest single contributor. Removing resolved classification admits **5**, because the broker then classifies the requested *name*, so a symlink called `cert.txt` needs no approval at all. Removing object-bound approvals while keeping exact request fields admits **2**, the retarget and stale-staged-state cases. So classifying the resolved object buys more than binding the approval to it, and having a tier at all buys more than either. A build that ships the binding and skips the tier has bought the smallest piece. **And the binding itself has a measured ceiling, which one further independent line measures and which this file did not state before v6.** Approval Laundering instruments one harness over 138 valid traces and reports, per class, the fraction of valid runs in which an effect outside the credential's own text occurs: **1.000** on scope, where a grant of `Bash(npm run test *)` is swept past by a `package.json` script's child process; **0.450** on arguments, where the agent issues the approved literal `git commit -m "wip"` unmodified in eight of its nine successes and a repository `pre-commit` hook running `git add -A` is what broadens the commit; and **1.000** on a same-name `$PATH` substitution under a grant of `Bash(cat *)`, where the rule, the command text and the recorded tool identity all match what was granted and only the resolved executable differs. An HMAC credential over principal, agent, session, tool, argument hash, scope and expiry, replayed paired over 118 of those runs, eliminates delegation (18 discordant to 0, p = 7.6e-6) and the seeded session mismatch (20 to 0, p = 1.9e-6) and moves scope **1.000 to 1.000** and arguments **0.450 to 0.400** (1 to 0, p = 1). The paper's own sentence is that "Tool Laundering is blocked" is false as a class-level statement, because `$PATH` resolution happens inside the dispatched process, below the mediation point, and no field the hook receives can reveal it. So the two classes a field-only credential cannot touch are exactly the two where every recorded field matches the grant, and the paper rules out the obvious repair in the same section: re-approving when an environment snapshot changes between grant and dispatch would have caught **none** of its cases, because in every one of them the hook or script is installed before the credential is issued. What it names instead is validating the effect the present environment would produce, at approval time, and it did not build that. **And the binding has a precondition as well as a ceiling, which v7 adds from the first of the three independent namings of this class, read in full here.** The two are invisible to the same check for opposite reasons. The ceiling is that the recorded fields agree and the operation differs, because the divergence sits below the mediation point. The precondition is that the recorded fields agree *because the record is lossy*, so the operation that differs was never in the record to compare against. The measured case is a gateway-mediated shell path at a released version: the positional argument vector was complete **before** approval, the product's approval event represented only the inline payload `$0 "$1"`, and additional positional arguments selected the command and destination that created the marker. The rendering was genuinely derived from the real command and was still a projection of it, so an allow-once decision released the complete vector and created the marker in **3 of 3** trials, with direct unapproved execution denied 3 of 3 in the same bundle and the unchanged approved command succeeding. This is why that paper's canonical approval record has **six** fields where the citing paper's summary of it has five. The sixth is a digest over the same complete descriptor shown to the human, with the rule that the display be generated from that descriptor and not from a lossy convenience string while execution uses a richer object. `digest` occurs **zero** times in the citing paper's 132,653 characters, checked here, and the citing paper maps the record onto its own seven-field credential, which has no such field. So the one field dropped in the summary is the one addressing a failure that credential cannot see, and the next release of the affected product, which checks the complete argument vector and rejects the mismatch 3 of 3 while still executing the approved command, is **the only released vendor fix for this class in any of the three namings**. **A second timing-shaped repair is also ruled out, now with two independent measurements and neither paper citing the other.** Refusing to persist approvals, or shortening the window, is not the fix: the negative control serialized, restored and resumed every approved action and executed it 3 of 3 and 3 of 3, while rejecting a same-call-ID raw mutation, so resumability is a carrier for post-approval substitution and neither necessary nor sufficient for it. What the control preserves is a canonical invocation fingerprint across serialization.

1. Generate the confirmation's text from the complete descriptor of the observed action at the enforcement point, and enumerate that surface's material fields first so you can tell complete from merely derived. Treat every string the model wrote or a fetched page supplied as adversary-chosen input, because a human shown a forgeable summary approves the disguised action at nearly the ungated rate. Then apply the completeness rule to your own renderer: a collapsed view may stay useful, but it must expose every material hidden field or refuse to authorize the action. On a shell surface check all six measured fields, inline payloads, positional arguments, environment changes, working directory, interpreter flags and redirections.
2. Bind the approval to the complete resolved operation, and at dispatch **reconstruct** that operation and compare it with the bound descriptor rather than re-checking the fields you stored. The five steps the source states, in order: refuse if the decision is missing, expired, revoked, consumed or outside its principal and task scope; refuse or re-ask if the current operation differs materially from the approved descriptor; re-evaluate current policy over the resolved effect including resource and destination; consume the decision atomically with the release of the effect, or record an idempotent commit token that prevents replay; and append the approved descriptor, the current descriptor, the decision and the effect to the audit record. Approving an action class leaves the substitution open. Then run an approval-time check of what the repository's committed hooks, its build and package scripts and the resolved `$PATH` would make that action do, and refuse a grant whose own text excludes an effect the environment is already configured to produce. Do not spend the budget on comparing environment snapshots between grant and dispatch instead, because that is the repair the measurement rules out.
3. Measure the two numbers that decide shippability: confirmations per legitimate task and false-block rate. The published floor is 0.94 and zero, so a design that costs several confirmations per task, or blocks legitimate work at all, has a defect rather than a tradeoff to accept.
4. Run a naive-summary arm beside your own gate rather than only an ungated arm. A gate that cannot beat its own naive arm by much more than six points is matching on adversary-chosen text (ours, not the paper's).
5. Port the decoder rather than the number. The 0 percent is measured where ground truth is a DOM commit; the shell-and-API decoder now exists and loses 2 of 74 where the browser arm lost none, and its 74 are attacks its own author wrote rather than a usage corpus, so carry the construction across and re-measure the rate on your surface.
6. Order the build by the ablation: the approval tier first, resolved-object classification second, object binding third. Shipping the binding without the tier buys 2 of 74 where the tier buys 18.
7. Audit your own resolver against an alias suite before trusting it, because its authors found **eight flaws in their own implementation, seven of them name-against-object gaps inside a system built to close name-against-object gaps**. The suite is the paper's own list of ways a path name and the opened file diverge: `..`, absolute paths, symlinks to files or to directories, hard links, case aliases on case-insensitive volumes, and a directory swapped between the check and the use. Resolution does not cover the last two on its own, and the paper is explicit about which mechanism does: a case alias such as `.ENV` is caught because the rules match without regard to case, not because the path was resolved, and the swap needs an `openat` walk with `O_NOFOLLOW` rather than a canonicalisation. So case-insensitive matching and a race-free open are each a separate requirement on the rule and the syscall, not properties a resolver supplies.

## Delta 2: the cost of the path is the uninspectable rate, not the latency

*Validation: none, same gap as delta 1. Eval tasks ap-t2, ap-t5, ap-t7. Both corpora here are third-party and were not authored for the paper, which makes this the best-evidenced section in the file and the one whose numbers transfer least, since they are properties of one trust list. The 2026-11-09 additions carry confidence intervals, which makes it the only part of this file where significance is stated rather than assumed. Four of its sentences were corrected across three readings on 2026-11-09, every one of them in the understating direction, and the paper has now been read in full three times by this seat and three by the research seat. That is provenance converging rather than validation, and this section still has no differential delta. The four-responses sentence and the 9-of-17 price rest on `2609.28586` alone, which carries no population dispersion anywhere and offers freeze discipline instead: a parser frozen before a disjoint prespecified holdout, an independent re-implementation that hash-checks 109 artifacts, and a four-profile denominator sensitivity in which one predeclared target drops to 0.778 against its own 0.80 bar. That is a different kind of warrant from delta 1's and not a weaker one, and the two papers are not interchangeable.*

The machine cost is not the constraint and the paper that measures it says so. Mean analyzer overhead is **0.056 ms**, median 0.050, p99 0.122, over n=4000 (Consent Integrity), which it calls negligible against inference latency, while stating plainly that this excludes the total mediation and trusted-path rendering its prototype specifies but does not implement and which "would dominate any end-to-end figure". Quoting the microsecond number as the cost of an approval path is therefore quoting the one component the authors exclude from the cost.

The cost it does measure sits on two corpora the authors did not write. On **1,330** trusted-tool abuse commands expanded from GTFOBins across 478 binaries, the analyzer flags **90.0 percent** and silently passes **10.0 percent**, and every silent pass runs through a binary its own trust list allows. On **28,798** idiomatic commands from tldr-pages across 6,503 tools, it prompts on **95.9 percent** and marks **87.0 percent uninspectable**, **83.4** of those percentage points purely because the tool is absent from the trust list, leaving **4.1 percent** that pass with no prompt. The paper names the trade-off as its central empirical finding and it is structural rather than an implementation gap: the same trust list that holds silent passes to 10 percent forces 87 percent uninspectable on normal use, growing the list to relieve that fatigue reintroduces silent passes because a trusted tool can be abused, and a boundary-only static mediator can choose where on the curve to sit but cannot occupy both ends.

**The mechanism behind the 87 percent is now corroborated on real sessions by an author arguing about something else.** Aegis OS replays 52 production coding-agent transcripts, 28,087 tool calls, and finds **39.0 percent of 11,070 real shell invocations (4,318) use compound operators or inline interpreter scripts**, rising to 80.5 percent in its dogfooding corpus. That is two independent corpora, one cause: what defeats a prefix decoder is the shape of ordinary shell use, not an exotic tail. Bounds: single author, and the corpus comes from one engineering organization.

**And there is now one measured route off the curve, which is the first evidence that the escape is real rather than argued.** Caper pre-executes each command in a snapshotted sandbox, hands an independent approver a bounded report of *observed* effects, commits on allow and rolls back on block. Against six systems on two benchmarks, one run per task, it reports **6.7 percent harmful on SABER and 7.8 percent on YoloFS-Ext at a 1.0 percent false-block rate**, where the deployed gate it compares against reads 14.8 and 62.7 at 3.9.

**Read the intervals before quoting it, because the two axes do not have the same status.** On harm the advantage is clean: Caper's YoloFS-Ext interval is [4.0, 14.7] against [53.1, 71.5] for the deployed gate, and on SABER [5.0, 9.1] against [12.2, 18.0], neither overlapping. **On false blocks it is a tie, and wider than an earlier version of this file said.** Caper's [0.2, 5.3] overlaps **three of the four ranked** comparators on that axis: the rule checker's [0.5, 6.9], the deployed classifier's [1.5, 9.7] and the strongest guardrail's [4.7, 15.9]. Only gpt-oss-safeguard-20b's [9.9, 24.0] is clean, so the separation is against one system and not against guardrails as a class. The fifth interval, Qwen3Guard's [0.0, 3.6], is discounted on **the paper's own authority rather than this file's**, because the dagger footnote under its main table reads "Blocks no command at all on YoloFS-Ext, dangerous or twin; not ranked". A reader who discounts it is citing the authors, and the ranked denominator is four. Carry that exclusion with its benchmark attached, since the same system blocks **10.0 percent of its 5,483 SABER decisions**, so "never blocks" is true of one benchmark and false as a property of the system. So the honest sentence is that a mediator consulting observed effects buys an order of magnitude on harm **at a false-block rate no worse than the best static rule checker's**, and the false-block *advantage* is unmeasured at this sample size. A skill claiming both axes would be claiming the half that is a tie.

**The sharpest finding in that paper is not a rate at all, it is a subtraction, and it is the one a reader should take.** Its appendix reports, per system, how often the command that caused the harm was blocked beside the twin false-block rate. Subtract the second from the first and you have how much each approver's blocking discriminates harm from its harmless twin. Pre-execution scores **+83.3** and the same approver as a hook **+60.7**. Every text-based approver scores **zero or negative**: the rule checker 2.0 against 2.0, the strongest guardrail 8.8 against 8.8, the deployed classifier 3.9 against 3.9, all three identical to the decimal, and gpt-oss-safeguard-20b **-1.0**, blocking the twin slightly more often than the harm. So those systems do block, and their blocks carry no information about which command was the dangerous one. **The bound is what makes it legible rather than what weakens it:** the twins carry an identical request and command by construction, so the subtraction measures discrimination only where the harm hides behind text the approver cannot tell apart, which is exactly the regime a text-based approver is in.

**Prediction is a different trade from the one an earlier version of this file described, and the correction matters because it reverses a decision rule.** That version read the predicted-effects arm as simply worse. With observed effects the twin false-block rate is 0.0, 0.0, 0.0 and 4.2 percent across the four concealment levels, with predicted effects 46.2, 57.7, 46.2 and 100.0, and the paper says in its own words that with prediction "harm drops as far as with observation". So prediction buys the whole harm reduction and pays for it in false blocks. **The paper then prints 61.8 percent twice, and that repetition is the finding.** It prints it once for the full-dialogue arm and once for dialogue-plus-predicted, because both pool to **63 of 102** twins, while command-only pools to the 52.0 the paper also prints and observed effects pool to **1 of 102**. So **prediction changes which twins are blocked and not how many.** Against the dialogue approver it replaces its cost is **0.0 points**. Against observation it is **60.8 points**, which is 61.8 against 1.0 and a subtraction rather than a figure the paper prints, so do not match it to the 60.8 printed elsewhere for Caper's safe-completion rate. The 61.8 is therefore the price of text-based approval and not the price of predicting. Two further corrections to how the row should be read. The **100.0** is an *abstention* and not a misjudgment: the predictor cannot read binaries, so where prediction is impossible it blocks every twin, which is a conservative failure and belongs in the procedure as one. And the per-level comparison against the full dialogue is correspondingly a **wash**, 46.2 against 65.4, 57.7 against 50.0, 46.2 against 50.0 and 100.0 against 83.3, which the pooled identity states exactly rather than by eye. The row prediction loses to on two of four is command-only, which differs from it by more than the prediction.

The paper's own reading of the whole ablation is that text-based approvers fail for lack of visibility rather than lack of strictness, and that judgment must come before the commit and from outside the agent, because the staging system that shows the same diffs to the agent afterwards has the agent that proposed the command decide whether to undo it.

1. Report the **uninspectable** rate and not the prompt rate. Some prompted commands are genuinely consequential, so the raw prompt rate flatters the design; uninspectable is the clean fatigue proxy and it is the paper's own choice of metric.
2. Measure silent passes on an abuse corpus and the uninspectable rate on a normal-usage corpus, both third-party. One corpus answers exactly one of the two questions, and a co-designed corpus answers neither.
3. Surface anything the decoder cannot classify as uninspectable or high-risk instead of approving it silently, so the guarantee is analyzer-relative and the gap is visible in the number rather than absent from it.
4. Price every trust-list addition as a silent-pass risk and record which abuse-corpus entries it admits, because 100 percent of the measured silent passes ran through trust-listed binaries rather than through a decoder gap.
5. Pick the point on the curve deliberately and write the number down. The way off the curve is a better decoder or mediation that is not boundary-only, never a longer trust list, and a design that has not named its point has defaulted to one.
6. If you go off the curve by consulting effects, consult **observed** effects, from a run in a sandbox you roll back, decided by an approver outside the agent loop before the commit. Price prediction against the baseline you are actually replacing, because the two available comparisons give opposite answers. Against a dialogue approver it is **free**, 61.8 percent of twins blocked either way, so if you already approve from text then adding a prediction costs nothing on this axis and buys the harm reduction. Against observation it costs **60.8 points**, 61.8 against 1.0, so if the choice is prediction or pre-execution then that subtraction is what pre-execution buys and it is the number to put in the decision. Observation is **1 twin of 102** rather than zero, so do not write it down as a free control. Prediction also abstains into a block on anything it cannot read, so budget that as a conservative failure. Where the separating information is simply absent, four responses are open and not one: represent the union of the possible effects, re-mediate, reject, or contain. The paper that derives the limit recommends the union, meaning bind a source-backed prediction of the transitive effect boundary into the approval object before authorizing, and its frozen holdout of 18 workflows over 18 unseen repositories prices that choice: macro recall rises 0.770 to **0.926** at 0.941 precision, case coverage 0.755 to **0.922**, residual effect instances 10 to **3**, and the bill is **9 of 17** reached cases routed to an extra approval that disclosed nothing new, with all seven newly disclosed effects landing in the positive stratum and control disclosure unchanged. Report that ratio beside the coverage gain, because a disclosure number alone hides it.
7. Price pre-execution's own exposure before shipping it. Pre-execution is a real execution, so network calls, writes to remote services and spawned processes are not undone by the rollback, which means the sandbox needs its traffic routed to a recording proxy rather than merely being trusted to be harmless.

## Delta 3: the allow-rule that relieves the fatigue is the measured escalation path

*Validation: none, same gap. Eval tasks ap-t3, ap-t6. This is the delta a bare subject is least likely to hold at all, and it is why this file is a containment document rather than a usability one: delta 2's remedy is delta 3's attack surface. The control behind this section was recounted on 2026-11-09 and is four times larger than the first reading of it; the correction is in REVISIONS.md.*

The standard remedy for delta 2's fatigue is the reusable allow-rule that Codex CLI and Claude Code both ship. Eliciting one during an ordinary benign task raises Permission Capability Coverage to **0.48 to 0.61** across four agent configurations, against **0.25 to 0.55** for a direct request, and makes **9 to 18 of 25** adversarial goals reachable (From Approval to Abuse). Initialized from those same checkpoints, downstream goal completion reaches **17 of 17** on one Codex configuration with 13 prefix-attributed, **14 of 18** on another with 11 prefix-attributed, and **13 of 14** on claude-haiku-4.5 with 9 prefix-attributed. On claude-sonnet-5, where the baselines fail completely at 0 of 9, the framework still realizes 3 of 9 through other authorized pathways with no prefix match at all.

The control is the number to design against, and this file previously understated it. The benign control is **0 of 25 per configuration across four configurations, so 0 of 100 pooled**, not the 0 of 25 an earlier version of this section quoted. A zero count is a bound rather than a rate, and the bound is what a design may rely on: **3.0 percent one-sided 95 percent upper bound pooled, 11.3 percent per model.** Pooling assumes the four configurations are exchangeable for this event, which is the arguable step, so quote both. That is still cheap enough to alert on, and it is a bound on a *synthesized* population: the control runs the paper's own 25 adversarially-paired tasks with the injection removed, in five categories chosen because they are dangerous, which is not the distribution of ordinary developer sessions. No published measurement gives the organic rate over an ordinary-usage corpus.

**And the remedy this attack rides on does not even deliver the relief it is sold for.** Replayed over 52 real sessions and 28,087 tool calls, a path-and-prefix allowlist of the usual development commands still prompts on **28.54 percent** of tool calls, against 53.31 percent for asking on every mutation. So the prefix rule buys roughly half the prompts while being, per this section, the thing the completed attacks were attributed through. Mechanisms that gate at the commit instead reach 4.97 and 1.12 percent on the same corpus, which is where the fatigue budget actually is.

1. Never create a persisted allow-rule from a scope the agent proposed mid-task. The scope comes from the authenticated request, compiled before any repository content or tool output is read.
2. Treat an in-task request to persist a rule as an alert with a mechanical response rather than a dialog, on the strength of the 0-of-100 pooled control. Size the alerting budget on the bound and not on the zero: at most 3.0 percent of benign runs pooled, 11.3 percent per model, and both numbers are bounds on a synthesized population, so measure your own rate before you tune a threshold against them.
3. Scope a persisted rule to the action and its resource, never to a command prefix, since the prefix is what the completed attacks were attributed through in 13 of 17, 11 of 14 and 9 of 13 of their cases.
4. Expire persisted rules at the task boundary and re-confirm, because the measured escalation crosses from acquisition to abuse on one unchanged checkpoint. If that costs too many prompts, gate at the commit rather than widening the rule: the same real-session corpus puts a commit-time gate at 4.97 percent and a scoped, time-limited lease at 1.12 percent.
5. Do not read model alignment as a defence on this path. The one model whose baselines scored 0 of 9 still gave up 3 of 9, by pathways that never matched the acquired prefix, so a stronger model moved the mechanism rather than the outcome.

So the rows now wait on the next distill run rather than on an account, and `status: draft` is expected to be the last thing this file needs.
- **The three founding papers each measure a different surface and none of them measures the others'.** The Verifiable Action Card is an agentic browser where ground truth is a DOM commit. Consent Integrity is a shell-command mediator whose total mediation and trusted path are specified and *not implemented*, which the authors say would dominate any end-to-end figure. From Approval to Abuse is coding agents on repository tasks. Reading delta 1's 0 percent onto a shell surface, or delta 2's 87 percent onto a browser, is the error this caveat exists to stop. **The two 2026-11-09 additions add a fifth and a sixth surface:** `2609.38983` is one coding-agent harness at one pinned model version, and `2609.28586` is developer toolchains at fixed commits, npm lifecycle, PEP 517, Cargo, Docker, git hooks and MCP. Neither reports a browser figure and neither is a usage corpus. **v7 adds a seventh surface and one prohibition.** `2609.21081` is agent-orchestration products and a gateway-mediated shell path, at deterministic denominators of 5/5 and 3/3 in frozen bundles rather than corpus rates, with no CWE, CVSS, severity or prevalence estimate anywhere in it and its own abstract saying the results do not estimate ecosystem prevalence. Two of its three positives are further bounded: the orchestration one has no fixed release and its exact-action control is research-authored rather than a product feature, and the other depends on a non-default authorization policy that a supported deny-update policy falsifies. **Never cite that paper for approval fatigue, dialog quality, deception rates or interface comprehension.** Its approval decision was scripted, and only after the test had asserted and recorded the exact product view, so it measures binding behaviour and says nothing about a human. It is the paper here whose method most resembles a human-factors study and the one that disclaims that reading twice in its own text, which is exactly where a reader in a hurry will reach for it. **The two constructions join at a direction, not at a measurement, and this file does not say the frontier is broken.** Object binding and pre-execution are different mechanisms reporting off-curve results on their own benchmarks. The measurement that would settle it is nameable and unrun: either construction scored on delta 2's own two corpora, 1,330 GTFOBins abuse commands and 28,798 tldr-pages normal-usage commands. It is queued.
- **Delta 2's percentages are properties of one trust list, not constants.** The 10.0 and 87.0 figures move with the list's size and composition; what transfers is the frontier's existence and the direction of the trade, which the paper argues structurally, rather than either endpoint.
- **Delta 3's denominators are small.** Twenty-five adversarial goals, four agent configurations, and downstream cases in the single digits to high teens. The benign control, 0 of 100 pooled, is the sturdiest number in that paper and the 17-of-17 is the most fragile, being one configuration at its ceiling. **An earlier version of this file read that control as 0 of 25 and so quoted a bound four times looser than the evidence supports, which is a correction in the conservative direction; it is recorded in REVISIONS.md because the alert in delta 3 rests on it.**
- **Delta 1's zero is across five open-weight models plus one 40-trial GPT-4.1 run**, so it is a strong result on a narrow model set, and the paper's claim is architectural rather than statistical: the check reads the ground-truth action after the model has decided, so it does not depend on the model's reasoning being robust.
- **The three 2026-11-09 additions are each bounded harder than their headline.** ZTAC's 74 attacks are authored by its own author, its utility workload is one small Python project, and it did not study reviewer fatigue or accuracy, so its 8 prompts in 46 operations is not comparable to delta 2's 87 percent on a usage corpus. Its own limitations say the handoff rules for files that other tools execute "have to be maintained like the list of credential files", so object binding changes what the list enumerates, from command strings to tool-handoff signatures, rather than removing the list. Caper runs each system on each task once, gates shell commands only and leaves file edits to the harness, describes one observed execution so a tool whose effects depend on time, randomness or the environment is uncovered, and uses an LLM approver and a partly-LLM oracle; neither of its benchmarks contains an attacker adapting to it. Aegis OS is a single author on one organization's traces, and its prompt rates are computed by replaying those traces through regimes rather than observed under them.
