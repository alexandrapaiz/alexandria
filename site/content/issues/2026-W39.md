# Harness distillation without the harness at runtime

*The latest in AI research, read in full and distilled weekly: what's new, what's gaining acceptance, and what newer evidence has overturned.*


You spent last week watching agents get faster by doing less at test time. This week the field asked a harder question: what if the scaffolding that makes an agent reliable could be taught to the model itself, then thrown away?

That is the thread running through the week's strongest results. A team from Peking University, Google, and HKUST found a way to distill an agent's external guardrails into the model so the deployed system needs none of them. Apple shipped a single visual agent that learns across desktop, mobile, and web without domain-specific tuning. And a regularized search method from Google Cloud and Stanford makes harness evolution less of a lottery. Three labs, one bet: the future agent carries its training structure inside it, not around it.

## The guardrails are becoming the model

The most consequential accumulation this week is around harness distillation—the idea that an agent's external control structure can be absorbed into the model weights and discarded at deployment.

A claim from Peking University, Google, and HKUST now has three independent papers building on it: that you can teach a smaller model to behave like a larger model supervised by an elaborate harness, then run the smaller model with minimal scaffolding. The original finding was that evolving a harness with a weaker model substantially improves domain-specific enterprise performance. This week the same group showed *how* to extract that benefit permanently. Their Harness-Zero pipeline raises base model success from 23.3% to 44.3% on a macro-average across spreadsheet work, multi-app tool use, and scientific reasoning—beating even the 41.7% achieved when the specialized harness stays attached at runtime. The key was not imitation learning from a stronger model's trajectories, but harness-guided review: an agent-as-harness checks each proposed action against a private reference, passes or replaces it, and the corrected trajectory becomes training data. Ablations were brutal: harness-guided review hit 30% macro-average success, while alternatives—raw stronger-model trajectories, trajectories without review, review without the private reference, review using only the final answer—managed 3% to 15%. That is a 10x gap in supervision quality, measured on the authors' own experiments across three domains, not yet replicated.

The behavioral analysis is what should change your design. The distilled model recovers 82.3% of the harness-induced behavioral patterns that the base model lacked, averaged across 28 identified patterns. The harness is not merely selecting better actions; it is teaching the model *how* to think in structured ways that persist without it. For builders, this reframes the deploy-time cost question: a heavy harness at training time may buy you a light runtime, and the tradeoff is now quantified.

Citation movement is thin but directional. The one-example distillation paper and the refusal-analysis paper each picked up a second citation, early signals that their respective communities are picking up the thread. The refusal work is worth watching separately: training on rationale-only supervision reduces false refusals while preserving safety, and three groups now report the same effect.

## Teaching the model to leave its scaffolding behind

The week's freshest work tests whether harness distillation can be made systematic rather than artisanal.

**A regularized search method makes harness evolution reproducible.** Researchers at Google Cloud AI Research, Stanford, and Washington University in St. Louis propose RRSI, a regularized recursive self-improvement loop for agent harnesses. The problem they address: unregularized harness evolution drifts, overfits, and bloats. Their solution layers three constraints on top of a standard evolutionary search. First, an annealed edit budget that shrinks following a cosine schedule, so early exploration is broad and late refinement is tight. Second, a selector that rejects candidates for benchmark leakage, statistical noise, or poor cost-to-gain ratio. Third, L1-style structural pruning that deletes harness components which have not produced strictly positive measured gain over a fixed window. The result: up to 14.1 points improvement on the evolution split, up to 4.7 points on five held-out benchmarks, and 30% fewer policy tokens than the unregularized baseline. The evidence is the authors' own experiments across eight benchmarks spanning coding, agentic workspace, and engineering design; no replication yet, and the OOD gains are modest enough that the method may be tightening rather than extending generalization.

**Apple's unified visual agent learns one policy for every screen.** A team at Apple trained MintAct, a single model that handles UI grounding, mobile navigation, desktop control, web browsing, and visual tool use without domain-specific heads. The architecture is a 2B/4B/8B vision-language-action model trained through multi-stage supervised fine-tuning: high-resolution single-step grounding, then low-resolution multi-step trajectories across all domains, then per-domain RL specialists, then rejection-sampling distillation back into a unified model, finally joint asynchronous RL with explicit cross-domain distribution balancing. The 8B model hits 48.9 on OSWorld-Verified, 39.1 on Online-Mind2Web, and 67.0 on AndroidWorld—matching or beating size-matched specialists on each. The critical finding for builders: joint RL training across heterogeneous domains outperforms RL specialists trained individually, and the asynchronous infrastructure stays stable under noisy feedback and off-policy drift with 200+ desktop and 100+ mobile environment instances running concurrently. This is Apple's own benchmark reporting; independent evaluation on these specific numbers is not yet available.

**A new benchmark tests whether agents can rebuild what they see.** Alibaba Group introduced RecreationWorld and its 250-task RecreationBench, split equally across Ubuntu, macOS, Windows, Android, and Web. The task: given a running application, rebuild it. Trajectories are long—median 282.5 top-level tool calls—with frequent switches between GUI interaction and code editing. The honest result: even GPT-6 Astra, the strongest tested, scores 58.1% overall but passes all programmatic tests on only 2.8% of tasks. Agents reproduce static interface structure more reliably than interactive behavior or computed outputs, and their reconstructions are smaller and more monolithic than the originals. The training signal is what matters for builders: fine-tuning on recreation trajectories yields up to 17.9 percentage-point gains on five out-of-distribution benchmarks covering coding, visual coding, and hybrid computer use. The evidence is Alibaba's own training runs; whether the benchmark itself becomes standard depends on adoption.

## The harness that was not enough

Start with the number that turned out to be wrong. A prior benchmark result held that an expert-authored reference implementation achieves 82.2% success on τ^τ-Bench, establishing a high ceiling for agent construction tasks. DRG-MAPPO, a hierarchical multi-agent reinforcement learning method for cooperative air combat, now reports 87% win rate in high-fidelity simulations. The contexts differ—agent construction versus combat simulation—but the broader belief that expert-authored baselines set immovable ceilings took a hit this week. The evidence is the DRG-MAPPO authors' own combat-domain experiments; whether the same relaxation holds for general agent benchmarks is untested.

The rest is not wrong so much as superseded. Several approaches that unified perception, interaction, and agentic control in monolithic models are being displaced by more modular designs. A programmable world model that separated state evolution from visual generation has been updated by SyncWorld's demonstration that a single visual-calibration episode enables zero-shot simulation in unseen environments. An embodied skills framework that combined high-level selection, low-level execution, and verification in one loop has been replaced by Show-Harness's finding that a compact semantic interface of discrete action units lets foundation vision-language models reason over and execute fine-grained robot actions without added model capacity. And the claim that self-evolving procedural graphs can repair flawed expert priors has been superseded by on-policy expert correction that rewrites only failing turns, preserving the weaker model's planning style rather than replacing it entirely. In each case, the newer work achieves comparable or better results with less architectural commitment, a pattern that should make builders skeptical of unified frameworks until the unification itself proves necessary.

## The hour you should spend

*Harness-Zero: Harness Distillation via Agent-as-Harness*, [full text](https://arxiv.org/html/2609.24974). Spend the hour if you are deciding whether to ship a heavy harness at runtime or invest in distilling it away; the 30% versus 3-15% ablation is the clearest cost-benefit analysis yet published.

*RRSI: Regularized Recursive Self-Improvement of Agent Harnesses*, [full text](https://arxiv.org/html/2609.24972). Worth it if your team has tried harness evolution and found it unstable; the cosine annealing and L1 pruning are directly extractable into existing pipelines.

*ACLArena: Agent Continue Learning in Multi-stage Post-training*, [full text](https://arxiv.org/html/2609.23989). Pick this up if you are stitching together SFT, RL, and distillation stages and need a framework for what to preserve and what to update at each transition.

Watch whether Harness-Zero's 44.3% holds when the harnessing agent itself is smaller than the student it supervises—that is the stress test that would make this the default pipeline. And watch whether MintAct's joint RL advantage survives outside Apple's infrastructure, because 200+ concurrent environments is not a budget every team has.
